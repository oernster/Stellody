Stellody

Stellar and melody, run together.

Free for Windows, macOS and Linux

The records you never knew
you were missing.

Stellody plays the collection already sitting on your computer, then works out what is not in it: albums by the artists you own that you never picked up, plus artists you have never heard who sit close to the ones you love.

All of it read off your own shelves rather than from a taste profile built by somebody else. Point it at your music folder and it does the rest. It is free, there is nothing to sign up for and it never changes a single one of your files.

What it finds

What your collection
is missing.

Tick the genres worth looking in. Stellody reads the artists already on your shelves, asks two public music catalogues what else those artists released and shows you the records you do not hold, along with artists who sit close to the ones you do.

It is your own collection asking the question, so the answer is not somebody else's idea of what you should like. There is no taste profile, no listening history and no account anywhere in it.

Free, on your own machine

The search runs on your computer and costs nothing. Nothing to sign up for, no key to get and no limit on how often you ask. Software that answers this question elsewhere tends to be a subscription.

The catalogues are named

MusicBrainz for what an artist released; ListenBrainz for which artists resemble another. Both answer without a key. What goes out is the artist names in the genres you ticked, never your files and nothing about you.

It says who it could not answer for

A name the catalogue holds nobody under is counted; so is one it holds several artists under. Either list can be opened. Without that, an answer that had missed a third of your collection would read exactly like a complete one.

Then to a shop, if you want one

Tick the albums you want and choose a shop: your browser opens that shop's own search for each one. Eight shops come with it and the list is yours to edit. Copy puts the ticked albums on the clipboard for anywhere else. No affiliation, no referral and nothing taken from a sale.

What one search found, turned a page at a time. Blue is an artist you hold; amber is one you do not. Every line says which it is in words.

How the search works

Your collection, sorted out

Real libraries
are messy.

Music you have collected over years is never tidy. Stellody works it out for you and shows one album wherever there ought to be one album. It is also what makes the missing records question answerable at all: a box set counted as four records looks like three gaps that are not there.

  • An album saved as one long file plays as its separate songs.
  • A box set spread over several folders comes back together as one record.
  • A bonus disc with no number in its name still lands in the right place.
  • Compilations keep every artist who played on them.
  • A bonus video shipped with the album plays as a track like any other.
  • Where a label is simply wrong, say what it should have been; your files stay untouched.
Everything it does
Why it can be trusted with your files

A player broke a library.
This one cannot.

Another player once quietly rewrote the song titles, artists and album names stored inside a real collection. It damaged 33 albums. They were all put right in the end; a program that thought it was helping should never have touched them.

Stellody is built the other way round. It opens your files to read them and never to change them. Where it finds something wrong it tells you plainly then leaves the file exactly as it found it.

  • Your music files are only ever read, never edited or moved.
  • Nothing is written into your music folder at all, not even a hidden cache.
  • Muddled track names are described to you so you can decide what to do about them.
  • Corrections Stellody works out can be kept or taken back, without a file being touched.
  • This is built into the program itself rather than left to good intentions.

What you get

Made for a collection
you spent years building.

A wall of album covers

Browse by artwork or as a plain list, whichever suits you; one button switches between them. Covers come in three sizes. Click one and the album opens underneath without losing your place. Stellody remembers how you like it.

Find anything as you type

Start typing and your collection narrows instantly, however many thousands of songs it holds. The song you were looking for lights up so your eye goes straight to it, with the rest of its album still around it where you expect to see it.

Tune the sound to your ears

Ten sliders take the bass up or the treble down; you hear the change as you move them. A row of little bars dances along the bottom of the window showing what the music is doing. Turn the sliders off and Stellody adds nothing whatever to the sound.

Narrow it by genre

Genres come from a settled list of eighteen, with sixteen styles under them, so a tick means the same thing every time rather than depending on how a label happens to be spelled. Tick more and you see more. Albums that state no genre at all have a box of their own, so nothing is stranded behind a label nobody filled in.

Albums that flow, flow

Some records are made to run straight through with no pause between the songs. Stellody plays them that way, with no silence dropped in where the artist never put one.

The track exactly as the file holds it

One press hands the sound device the track exactly as the file holds it, with the system mixer out of the way. A line beside the time says what the device actually took, including "bit perfect" where that is true. Offered on Windows and macOS for any lossless song at a rate the device takes.

Worth knowing before you download

What it does not do.

It does not play every format

FLAC, MP3, Ogg, Opus, WAV, AIFF, M4A, WMA, WavPack and AAC are read. Monkey's Audio, Musepack, DSD, TAK, TrueAudio, CAF and M4B audiobooks are not, so that is the one to check before you download. A file in one of those formats is named in its health report, as is one whose details cannot be read, so nothing goes missing without a word.

Windows, macOS and Linux

A setup program on Windows, a signed disk image on macOS and a Flatpak on Linux. Out of the box the sound reaches your speakers through the system mixer, which converts on the way, so it is not bit perfect. Exclusive output takes the mixer out of the way on Windows and macOS; Linux does not offer it.

Free, with one quiet ask

No price, no adverts and no subscription. There is one donate button, at the left end of the strip along the bottom, ruled off from the controls beside it so it is not pressed by accident; its tooltip offers to buy the author a drink.

Stellody is written and looked after by one person, so that button is how the work gets paid for and it is the whole of the asking: no dialog, no reminder, no nagging later, nothing counting how long you have gone without. Nothing about the program changes if you never press it. Pressing it hands an address to your web browser.

It is a player, nothing more

Stellody does not stream, does not copy your CDs, does not sync to your phone and will not tidy up your files by rewriting them. It plays what you already have.
