Getting your music in

It reads your collection once

Add music later without the wait

Choose your music folder once. After adding an album, press Rescan: Stellody reads only what changed, so it finishes in a moment.

It tells you what it found

A finished scan gives a report: the new albums by name, how many tracks came with them and the totals for your whole collection. Albums no longer found are listed too, since a renamed folder looks just like a deleted one. A scan that finds nothing new says so.

One folder, one album

Stellody trusts how your music is filed. Grouping by the labels inside the files split one Mozart recording into five albums, so folders decide.

Box sets come back together

A release filed as CD1 and CD2 (else Disc 1 and Disc 2) shows as the one record it is.

Nothing goes missing quietly

Stellody skips only a short, fixed list of folders that operating systems, network drives and version control tools create. It never guesses that something looks unimportant: an early version did and swallowed two real albums.

FLAC, MP3, Ogg, Opus, WAV, AIFF, M4A, WMA, WavPack and AAC

Stellody treats every format alike: a folder of MP3s becomes an album exactly as a folder of FLACs does, with titles, artists, dates and track numbers read from whatever each file carries.

Cover art inside a FLAC, MP3, WAV, AIFF or M4A file is read too. Monkey's Audio and a handful of rarer formats are named in the health report rather than skipped.

WMA, WavPack and AAC rest on weaker evidence: with no real files to test against, each is proved against a file the test suite encodes. WMA and AAC are lossy and state no bit depth; WavPack is lossless and keeps its depth.

The bonus video comes in too

A video shipped with an album scans as a track of that album and plays like any other, so the album holds as many tracks as the disc it came from.

When the labelling is muddled

It tells you, then leaves it alone

Every music file carries labels: the track name, its number, the album, the artist. On a collection built over years some go wrong. Stellody works out what was meant so the album reads correctly, tells you what looked odd and leaves your file untouched.

Two songs claiming to be track 4

The number at the front of the file name wins; file names stay right where labels go wrong.

A disc number that disagrees with the folder

The folder wins: a person named it, while software wrote the label. One real folder holds songs claiming three different discs.

The same song title twice

The file name wins again; bulk relabelling tends to copy the title along with the number.

You get the full list

Everything Stellody worked around is listed in one place, so you can fix it properly in whichever program you like.

Or tell it to keep what it worked out

Accept the whole list in one press or an album at a time; those corrections then stop being reported. Take back one group of files, one album or the lot in a single press and the findings return. Your files are untouched either way.

Or say what it should have been

Where a label is simply wrong, state the album artist, title, date or genre yourself. What you state overrides both the labels in the files and anything Stellody works out.

One track can be corrected the same way (its title, artist, disc number or track number), even on an album held as one file with a cue sheet. Nothing is written into a music file, so any of it can be taken back.

Joining two folders by hand

A stated label is what an album is recognised by, so giving one folder the artist and title another carries makes them one album: the way to join a release held in two places under two spellings. The joined album keeps the rating held against the one it joined.

Finding your way around

Two ways to look, one way to search

A list, else a wall of covers

Read your collection as a list or browse it as covers in three sizes. Click a cover and the album opens beneath it, its songs in two columns, without losing your place; click again to close it.

A title too long for its tile ends in an ellipsis; rest the pointer on it to see the whole.

Switching keeps your place

Moving between the list and the covers lands you where you were, with whatever is playing picked out and its album open.

Open every album at once

The list shows one row per album, opened to its songs by the chevron beside it. The chevron at the left of the Title heading opens or closes them all at once.

Albums in the order you would expect

Each artist's albums run from first to latest. A leading The is set aside, so The Police files under P. Compilations marked Various, VA or Various Artists show as Various Artists, run by title with numbers counted as numbers, so #2 comes before #10. Sort Z to A on the View menu reverses the order.

It narrows as you type

Open the search box and your collection narrows with every letter; close it and everything returns.

Instant on a real collection

Over eight thousand songs are searched in about a millisecond and a third, with nothing to build first or wait for.

Albums stay whole while you search

Find one song and its album stays whole around it; the song lights up and flashes so your eye goes straight to it.

The list view, with the play controls across the top and your settings along the bottom.

Narrowing it down

Genres from a settled list

Left alone a collection states dozens of genre labels, many spelling one idea different ways. Stellody offers a fixed list instead, so a tick means the same thing every time.

Twenty-one genres, thirty-six styles under them

Fifty-seven boxes in all, the genres in alphabetical order with each one's styles beneath it; an album can carry several. Names follow Discogs' spellings where it has them, so a label written by another program has a chance of matching. Electronic music splits into Dance, Electronic, House and Techno & Electro, with styles for the names MusicBrainz gives suggested artists. Each genre's styles fold away behind an arrow; every dialog remembers which you left open.

Ticking more shows more

Rock and Jazz together gives you both, so every tick widens what is on screen.

A style means that style

Tick Deep House for deep house alone; tick House for every kind, deep house included.

Albums with no genre are reachable

More than a tenth of a real collection states no genre, so those albums have a box of their own. An album labelled with something off the list lands there too.

It works with the search, not against it

A narrowed collection can still be searched and a search can still be narrowed; the two simply compose.

Years too

Below the genres sit Discover's two year fields, released from and to. Only albums dated within them are shown; an undated album is left out while years are set. Years alone are enough. A year that cannot be used is flagged beneath the fields and Show waits until it is fixed; Clear empties them with the ticks.

Your taste profiles here too

A Taste profiles button beside Clear offers the profiles kept in Discover, ticking their genres and setting their years. Pressing Show with genres no profile holds offers to keep them as a new one. It is one shared list, so a profile deleted here is gone there too.

Keeping your own opinion

Ratings and play counts

Five stars for a song

Stars sit in a Rating column beside each song's play count, in the list and the open album alike, so you can read a record's ratings at a glance and rate without listening first. Press a star to rate; press the same star to clear. With a song highlighted, keys 1 to 5 rate it and 0 clears it.

Albums get their own rating

Set from the album's own labelled stars. Neither rating is calculated from the other, because one weak track does not make a bad record.

Only a finished song counts

Skipping adds nothing, so the numbers mean something. Each song shows its own count, so you can see what you keep coming back to.

Your ratings survive a tidy-up

Rename or move a folder and ratings and play counts follow the album. Album edits and track corrections stay with the folder path, so a moved folder shows its automatic values until it returns or the edits are restated. None of it is written into your files.

Album art

Your covers, plus help finding the rest

It uses the artwork you already have

Whether the cover is a picture file beside your music or inside the songs, Stellody finds it. An album with no artwork gets a plain square.

You choose, it never guesses

For an album with no artwork, ask Stellody to look. It shows every cover found, labelled by edition; you pick. It never attaches a sleeve on its own.

The cover you picked stays picked

A cover you chose stays chosen; rescanning will not put the old one back.

Busy is not the same as nothing

The public archives are often busy or slow. A search, a listing or a picture that fails in passing (a server error, a refusal or a timeout) is asked again, waiting longer each go. An archive saying a release has no picture is an answer; that is never asked twice.

A search still refused or never answered says so plainly rather than claiming your album has no artwork. Quitting while a lookup runs can take a few seconds longer as it winds down.

Keeping it safe

Back it up, put it back

Ratings, play counts and corrections cannot be recovered by rescanning. Back up library on the File menu keeps them; Restore from backup puts them back. The Guide walks through both.

One zip holds what Stellody knows

A backup holds the library's records (ratings, play counts, corrections, stated tags, settings and taste profiles) with what discovery has learned: the catalogue memory, the last answer, the genre cache and the shop list. It leaves out the music, which is yours to copy, as well as the cover and waveform pictures, which Stellody redraws.

Wherever you keep it

You choose the folder: Downloads the first time, then wherever the last backup went. Each is named for the minute it was made, so none replaces another. Music keeps playing while it is written.

Restoring asks first

Restore from backup checks the zip, then asks before replacing your library. It first backs up the library as it stands, then restarts Stellody to put the restored one in place; if that fails part way, the library you had is put back.

Old backups always restore

A backup from an older version always restores, brought up to date as it goes in. One holding a file in a shape newer than this version understands is refused, with the reason.

Moving to another computer

Restored on a computer whose music sits somewhere else, Stellody says the music folder is not there and offers to choose one.
