Why

A player broke a library.

Someone spent years turning a shelf of CDs into files: ripping each one, checking the track names, fixing the artist on the compilations, finding the right cover art. Then a well known music player reached into those files and rewrote the information stored inside them. It duplicated and overwrote the details on 33 albums. The music still played; the careful work around it was gone.

Stellody was written because of that afternoon. It is built on one rule that everything else follows from: your files are opened to be read and never to be changed. If Stellody finds something muddled in the way an album is labelled, it tells you and leaves the file alone. Deciding what to do about it stays your job, in whatever program you like.

The promise

Not a promise.
A rule the program cannot break.

Anybody can write "we never touch your files" on a website. It costs nothing to say and nothing to quietly stop being true a year later. So in Stellody it is not a sentence. It is checked automatically every single time the program is built; if it ever stopped being true the build would simply fail.

Your music files are only ever read

The part of Stellody that could write to a file is kept out of reach of every part that opens your music. Not by care, by construction.

Nothing is put in your music folder

No stray database, no hidden cache, no little files left behind. Everything Stellody learns about your collection is kept in its own place, well away from yours.

Nothing quietly reaches the internet

Only two small pieces of Stellody are even capable of making a connection; both are named below. Playing your music uses neither.

Your library survives a mistake

An unplugged drive or a half finished scan never makes Stellody forget your albums. Missing files are flagged for you rather than thrown away.

Your privacy, in plain words

Nothing about you
ever leaves this program.

Stellody does not know who you are. There is no account to make, no profile, no newsletter and no record kept anywhere of what you listen to. Nobody is told what is in your collection. Your music plays perfectly well with the internet switched off altogether.

Three things reach outside your computer at all. Rather than leave you to discover them, here are all three.

Looking for album art

Only ever when you ask for it, on one album at a time. Stellody looks that album up in a public music archive then shows you the covers it found so you can pick one.

Checking for a new version

Once a day Stellody asks whether a newer version has come out. It sends nothing whatever when it asks, not even which version you have; it reads one small public page then compares it on your own machine. It stays quiet unless there is something new. You can tell it to skip a version for good.

The donation button

If you press it, Stellody hands the address to your web browser and your browser opens the page. Stellody itself connects to nothing.

Worth knowing

What it is not.

Stellody plays FLAC files, which is the format people use for copies kept at full CD quality. If your collection is MP3, M4A or WMA it will not find it yet. That is the one thing worth checking before you download, so it is said here rather than buried. Widening it is being worked on.

It is a player and only a player. It does not stream, does not copy your CDs for you, does not sync to a phone and will not reorganise your collection by rewriting your files. It can tell you what looks muddled in an album's labelling, though it cannot yet tidy that up on your behalf. Where a button is not ready, it is greyed out rather than left to disappoint you.

Windows is the only version today. Mac and Linux are planned.

For anyone who wants the detail

Under the bonnet.

You do not need any of this to use Stellody. It is here because the claims above are the kind that are easy to make and hard to check, so this is the working behind them. The code is public and you are welcome to go and look.

Every guarantee is a test

The rules above are enforced by tests that fail the build. Each of those tests was itself checked by deliberately breaking the rule and watching it fail, because a guard nobody has seen fail is not yet a guard.

A track is a slice of a file

In the collection Stellody was built against, 163 of 482 albums come from a single file with a cue sheet rather than one file per track. That is a main path rather than an edge case, so it is captured once in the core idea instead of special-cased all over the program.

Search has no index

A full text index was the first plan and measurement refused it: reading the whole library on every keystroke costs under half a millisecond, against the hundred and twenty a typed character allows. An index would also have held the wrong text.

Folders group, tags name

Grouping albums by their tags was tried against a real collection and failed: it split one Mozart folder into five albums, two holding a single track. A folder boundary is what a ripper actually recorded, so that is what is trusted.

Measured rather than assumed

The waveform used to be folded a frame at a time and took 3.2 seconds on a 60 megabyte track; done over blocks it takes 0.84, for bit for bit the same answer. Numbers like these decided the design rather than opinions about them.

The rules stay clean

The layer holding the library rules touches no files, no network and no clock; even the time arrives as an argument. It is held at 100% branch coverage, so nothing in it goes unexercised.
