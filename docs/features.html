Everything Stellody does.

The whole list, in plain words. Where something is not built yet, it is said here rather than left out and discovered later.

Finding more of it

Music you do not own yet

The rest of this page is about the music already on your disk. This is the opposite question: given what you have collected, what is worth reaching for next. Tick the genres worth looking in and Stellody asks two public music catalogues what those artists released that you do not hold, along with who else sounds like them.

What the search found, turned a page at a time. Blue is an artist you hold; amber is one you do not. Every line says which it is in words.

You choose what it looks at

The genres you tick decide which of your artists are named, so what goes out is a subset you chose rather than an inventory of everything you own. What goes out is those artist names, the identifiers the catalogue gives back for them and for the similar artists it suggests, the fixed settings each request states for itself plus a user agent naming Stellody, its version and the project's contact address. The language asked for is fixed at any rather than your computer's own: no collection, no counts, nothing about you or your machine.

Compilations, if you want them

A compilation filed under Various Artists is one name that means nobody, so by default it adds nothing. Tick the box for compilations and the search asks about the artists on their tracks instead, still only in the genres you ticked; a line beneath the box says how many of those artists have never been looked up and roughly how many minutes they add.

A credit naming two artists is looked up whole first, then as its two names only if nobody answers to the whole.

It says how it is getting on

A search takes minutes, because the catalogues ask to be questioned slowly and Stellody keeps to that. Two bars in the toolbar show each half of it, hovering names who is being asked about and the time remaining is written both on the bar and along the foot of the window.

Stop means stop

While a search runs, the button that started it wears a cross and offers to stop it. One press ends it there and then, with nothing to confirm: the request already in flight is dropped rather than waited out.

What it found, read plainly

Artists you hold are shown in one colour with the albums by them you do not underneath; artists you hold nothing by are shown in another; opening one fetches its records then. Every line also says in words which kind it is, so the colours are never carrying the meaning alone.

A page at a time, up to three columns wide

A whole library answers with hundreds of artists, which is more list than anybody reads. The answer is dealt across the width of the screen and turned a page at a time, with two controls under it saying which page of how many you are on; the direction that leads nowhere wears its own picture struck through.

Albums you tick stay ticked as you turn the pages, so what goes to a shop is everything you picked out wherever you picked it.

Narrow a long answer by genre

A search over a whole library can answer with thousands of albums. A Filter button under the answer offers the genres that search looked in; tick some and the pages are dealt again from what is left. An artist you hold stays where one of your own albums by them sits under a picked genre.

A similar artist stays where an earlier search recorded what they play, else is held back with a line saying how many. Filtering never takes a tick away, while Copy and Find in shops act only on the ticked albums you can see.

A search always shows you what it found

One artist a catalogue would not discuss no longer costs you the rest: what could not be answered for is written down beside what was; the screen opens either way, saying how many artists are missing from it.

Every answer is kept the moment it arrives too, so a search stopped, interrupted or cut off by the machine going down loses nothing it had already paid for. Running it again asks only for what is still missing, which is why a second attempt is quick.

It never offers you what you already have

A remaster of a record you own is the same record, so it is not a gap; a live album of the same name is a different one, so it is. That rule was worked out against a real collection rather than assumed; the edition wording rippers leave in titles is stripped from both sides before anything is compared.

It says who it could not answer for

Some questions come back with nothing usable: the catalogue is having a bad moment; it holds nobody under a name; it holds several artists under one and none of your tags says which is yours.

Each kind is counted separately at the end of a search, since one is worth trying again later and one is a spelling worth a look. A button beside that count opens the names themselves. Without it, an answer that had missed a third of your collection would read exactly like a complete one.

Then to a shop, if you want one

Tick the albums you want and choose a shop: your browser opens that shop's own search for each one. Stellody has no affiliation with any of them, no arrangement and no referral; it takes nothing from a sale and sells nothing itself. Copy puts the ticked albums on the clipboard for anywhere else.

The shops screen edits its own list: add a shop, change or delete one, move it with Ctrl and the arrow keys or drag it by its grip, which carries the shop with the pointer while the others slide aside.

A Try button opens a new shop's search before you keep it and a shop that cannot search will not save. When a new version changes the shops it ships, a shop you deleted stays deleted and one you changed keeps your change; one press puts the original shops back while keeping any you added.

Tick what to look in. Find closes this window; the search then reports from the toolbar.

Choosing a shop opens that shop's own search for each ticked album. The window stays open so prices can be compared; the list itself is yours to change.

Getting your music in

It reads your collection once

Add music later without the wait

Choose your music folder once. When you add an album afterwards, press Rescan: Stellody looks only at what has actually changed, so it finishes in a moment rather than reading everything again.

It tells you what it found

When a scan finishes you get a report rather than a message that vanishes: the new albums by name, how many new tracks came with them, then the totals for your whole collection. Albums that have stopped being found are listed too, since a folder you renamed or moved looks just like one that has gone. A scan that finds nothing new says exactly that.

One folder, one album

Stellody trusts how your music is actually filed. Sorting by the labels inside the files was tried and it fell apart on classical music, splitting a single Mozart recording into five different albums. Your folders know better, so your folders decide.

Box sets come back together

A double album filed as CD1 and CD2, else as Disc 1 and Disc 2, shows up as the one record it really is rather than as two strangers sitting next to each other.

Nothing goes missing quietly

Stellody skips a short, fixed list of folders that operating systems, network drives and version control tools create for themselves. It never guesses that something looks unimportant, because an early version guessed and swallowed two real albums.

FLAC, MP3, Ogg, Opus, WAV, AIFF, M4A, WMA, WavPack and AAC

A collection built over years is rarely all one format, so Stellody reads the common ones and treats them alike: a folder of MP3s becomes an album exactly as a folder of FLACs does, with the same titles, artists, dates and track numbers read out of whatever each file happens to carry.

Cover art stored inside a FLAC, MP3, WAV, AIFF or M4A file is read too, including the cover an M4A keeps in its own Apple atom. What is left, Monkey's Audio and a handful of rarer formats, is named in the health report rather than quietly skipped.

WMA, WavPack and AAC arrive on a weaker footing than the rest and it is worth saying which. Every other format here was tested against files somebody owns; there were none of those three, so each is proved against a file the test suite encodes for the purpose. WMA and AAC are lossy, so neither states a bit depth; WavPack is lossless and keeps the depth it states.

The bonus video comes in too

Albums that shipped a video alongside the songs used to have it left out silently: not a track, not a note in the health report, just absent, so the album quietly held fewer tracks than the disc it came from. Those files now scan as tracks in the albums they belong to and play like any other track, with the controls, the queue and the position bar reaching them exactly as they reach a song.

When the labelling is muddled

It tells you, then leaves it alone

Every music file carries labels saying what it is: the track name, its number, the album, the artist. On a collection built over years, some of those go wrong. Stellody works out what was probably meant so your album still reads correctly, tells you what looked odd then leaves your file untouched.

Two songs claiming to be track 4

The number at the front of the file name wins. Whenever labels have gone wrong, the file names themselves have stayed right.

A disc number that disagrees with the folder

The folder wins. A person named that folder deliberately; the label inside was written by software. One real folder holds songs claiming to be from three different discs.

The same song title twice

The file name wins again. When something rewrites labels in bulk it tends to copy the title across along with the number.

You get the full list

Everything Stellody worked around is written down for you in one place, so you can go and correct it properly in whichever program you like.

Or tell it to keep what it worked out

Accept the whole list in one press or one album at a time; those corrections then stop being reported every time you start. Changed your mind? Take back one group of files, one whole album or the lot, each in a single press; the findings they answered come back. Your files are untouched either way: the decision is kept in Stellody's own records.

Or say what it should have been

Where a label is simply wrong rather than muddled, state it yourself: the album artist, the title, the date and the genre. What you state is what the album is from then on, over both the labels in the files and anything Stellody would work out for itself.

One track can be put right the same way: its title, artist, disc number or track number, even on an album held as one file with a cue sheet. Nothing is written into a music file; it is kept in Stellody's own records like everything else, so it can be taken back at any time.

Joining two folders by hand

Because a stated label is what an album is recognised by, giving one folder the artist and title another already carries makes them one album. That is the way to join a release your collection happens to hold in two places under two spellings. The joined album keeps the rating held against the one it joined.

Finding your way around

Two ways to look, one way to search

A list, else a wall of covers

Read your collection as a tidy list, else browse it as album art in three different sizes. Click a cover and the album opens underneath it with its songs in two columns, taking no more room than they need, so you never lose your place. Click it again to close it.

Switching keeps your place

Move between the list and the covers and you land where you were. Whatever is playing is picked out in the view you arrive at, with its album already open, exactly as though you had got there that way in the first place.

Open every album at once

The list shows one row per album, opening to its songs when you press the chevron beside it. One press on the chevron at the left of the Title heading opens all of them; another closes them again. It is instant on a collection of any size.

It narrows as you type

Open the search box, start typing and your collection narrows with every letter. Close it and everything comes back.

Instant, however much music you have

Every song in your collection is searched in a millisecond or two, far faster than you can type. There is nothing to build first and nothing to wait for.

Albums stay whole while you search

Find one song and its album stays as it always looks, with every other track still around it. The song you were after lights up and flashes so your eye goes straight to it.

The list view, with the play controls across the top and your settings along the bottom.

Narrowing it down

Genres from a settled list

Left to itself a collection states dozens of different genre labels, several of them spelling the same idea different ways. Stellody offers a fixed list instead, so what you tick means the same thing every time.

Eighteen genres, sixteen styles under them

Thirty-four boxes in all, in alphabetical order. An album can carry several at once. The names are Discogs' own, so they are ones you have seen before and ones a label written by another program has a chance of matching.

Ticking more shows more

Asking for Rock and Jazz at once gives you both rather than nothing, so every tick widens what is on screen instead of narrowing it to the albums that somehow answer to everything.

A style means that style

Tick Trance and you get trance, not everything electronic. Tick Electronic and you get the lot, trance included, because Stellody already knows which main each style belongs to.

Albums with no genre are reachable

There is a box for them, because more than a tenth of a real collection states no genre at all and no other tick could ever reach those albums. An album labelled something the list does not carry is in the same box, for the same reason.

It works with the search, not against it

A narrowed collection can still be searched and a search can still be narrowed. Neither knows about the other, so the two simply compose.

Playing

Playing your music

Start it however you like

Double click a song, press Return, use the right click menu, the buttons across the top or the play button on an open album. Stellody carries on through the album by itself.

You can always see what is playing

The song playing is marked on its row in the list and in the album open under the covers, even while another song is selected; the mark moves with Next, Back and one song running into the next. Its title and artists sit along the foot of the window for as long as it is loaded: pausing keeps them, stopping clears them.

Back goes to the start first

Press Back once to return to the beginning of the song you are on. Press it again to go to the one before, the way it works on a CD player.

Volume, mute, shuffle and repeat

They sit at the right end of the strip along the bottom: volume, mute, the output device, exclusive output and the equalizer, then shuffle and repeat. Volume, mute, shuffle and repeat are all remembered for next time. Repeat has three settings rather than two: off, repeat the album, then hold the one song you are on.

Shuffle reorders the album afresh each time it comes round again. Every switch shows you what pressing it would do rather than what it is doing now, so you never have to work out which way round it is.

A song that will not play says why

A drive that is not plugged in, a file in a format this build cannot decode, an output device another program has taken exclusively: any of them used to look exactly like a press that missed the button. Stellody names the song and the reason along the bottom of the window instead, hands the sound device back and leaves the rest of your library working.

Choose where the music plays

A button on the bottom strip, also Output device on the Sound menu, lists System default first and then every device your computer can play through, in its own order, with two devices of one name numbered apart. Choose one and the song moves there from where it was; a paused song stays paused. The choice is remembered, while the list keeps up as devices come and go.

Headphones you chose that disconnect pause the music; press play and it carries on through the system default, while the list names them as not connected with System default ticked. Put them back on and the music moves back to them by itself. A device that will not open leaves the music on the system default, with the bottom of the window naming it and the reason it gave. Tried on Windows, macOS and Linux, every listed device chosen and heard playing.

Headphones on, music follows

With System default chosen, headphones that connect and become your computer's sound output take the song with them, from where it was, with no press. Headphones switched off pause the music rather than sending it out of the speakers: both play buttons turn back to play at once and the bottom of the window says why. Press play and the song carries on through the new output from the point you last heard.

Albums that flow, flow

Live recordings, concept albums and most dance records are made to run straight through with no pause between the songs. Stellody plays them that way, with no silence dropped in where the artist never put one.

See the shape of the song

A picture of the whole track runs along the bottom, showing the quiet parts and the loud ones at a glance, with a line marking where you are; beside it, the time reached and the length of the whole track. Click anywhere on it to jump there. A track played before draws its shape at once; a new one is measured the moment it is highlighted, before you press play.

No static on a busy computer

Music used to break up into static while the computer was working flat out on something else, such as a large software build using every core. Stellody now keeps a larger reserve of sound queued at the output device, so a moment's delay in feeding it is not heard.

The fix was measured and heard on Windows; macOS and Linux ask their mixer for the same reserve. Sound now leaves the device a fraction of a second after it is decoded, which the position line and the dancing bars allow for.

The sound itself

Tune it, then watch it move

Ten sliders, from deep bass to high treble

Open it from its button at the right end of the bottom strip, else from the Sound menu. Take the bass up, bring the harshness down, then hear the change as you move each slider. A lift is given room rather than clipped: the whole curve is brought down by the most it raises any part of the sound, so a record already mastered loud never distorts.

That is why music plays quieter with a lift switched on; a curve that only cuts keeps its own level. There is a separate on and off switch, so you can compare it against the untouched sound without losing the settings you spent time on.

Switched off means genuinely untouched

Plenty of players still push your music through their sound processing even with everything set to zero. Stellody adds nothing of its own: turned off, the equalizer hands the block back untouched rather than multiplying it by one. Left at full, the volume does the same.

Out of the box, what leaves Stellody then goes through your system's sound mixer, which converts on the way, so it is not bit perfect. Exclusive output, below, takes the mixer out of the way.

Exclusive output, exactly as the file holds it

One press on the bottom strip asks the sound device for the music with the system mixer out of the way, which is the only way it can be bit perfect. The song in hand reopens where it was; a paused song stays paused. Beside the time along the bottom, a line says what the device actually took: the mode, the rate, the depth, plus "bit perfect" where that is true. Bit perfect also needs the volume at 100% and the equalizer off, since either one alters the samples.

It is offered only for a song the device can take untouched: not for a lossy song such as an MP3, which has nothing to deliver untouched, nor at a rate the device does not take. A Bluetooth headphone taking 48 kHz alone cannot have a 44.1 kHz CD rip that way. For such a song the switch is shut off with the reason on it, naming the rates the device does take; your choice stands, so the next song that can have it gets it without a press. With no song selected the device is judged against your library instead.

Changing the sound output asks the new device afresh. A device that turns the request down, because another application holds it say, takes the switch back to shared and the foot of the window says why. On Windows the device is handed over outright; on macOS it runs at the song's own rate and refuses to convert, though another application playing at the same time is still mixed in. Linux does not offer it.

Little bars that dance

Twenty bars along the bottom of the window move with whatever is playing. They line up with the ten sliders, two bars to a slider, so you can see exactly which control owns the part of the sound you are listening to.

Turning the volume down does not shrink them, because they are showing you the music rather than the volume. They measure the sound after the equalizer, so with a lift switched on they mostly read lower than the same record with it off.

It cannot change what you hear

The bars watch the sound only after it has already gone to your speakers, so they cannot alter it even by accident. There is no switch and nothing to set up: it is simply there; it stops completely when the music does.

Keeping your own opinion

Ratings and play counts

Five stars for a song

The stars sit in a Rating column on every song's row, beside its play count, in the list and in the album open under the covers alike. You can read a whole record's ratings at a glance and rate something without listening to it first. Press a star to rate the song; press the star it already sits on to clear the rating. With a song highlighted, the number keys 1 to 5 rate it and 0 clears it.

Albums get their own rating

Set from the stars on the album itself, labelled so you cannot mix the two up. Neither is calculated from the other, because one weak track does not make a bad record.

Only a finished song counts

Skipping through an album adds nothing to your counts, so the numbers mean something. Each song carries its own count on its row, so you can read down an album and see what you keep coming back to.

Your ratings survive a tidy-up

Rename or move a folder and your stars and counts follow the album rather than getting lost. Neither is ever written into your music files.

Album art

Your covers, plus help finding the rest

It uses the artwork you already have

Whether the cover sits beside your music as a picture file or is tucked inside the songs themselves, Stellody finds it and remembers it. An album with no artwork gets a plain square rather than an ugly gap.

You choose, it never guesses

For an album with no artwork, ask Stellody to go and look. It shows you every cover it found, each labelled with the edition it came from; you pick. It will never quietly attach the wrong sleeve to your favourite record.

The cover you picked stays picked

Once you have chosen a cover it is yours. Rescanning your collection will not quietly put the old one back.

Busy is not the same as nothing

The public archives Stellody asks are often busy. It tries several times, waiting a little longer each go, then tells you plainly that it could not get an answer rather than pretending your album has no artwork anywhere.

Living on your desktop

The window and the tray

It opens where you left it

Same size, same place, still maximised if that is how you left it. Unplug the big monitor and Stellody still opens somewhere you can actually reach it.

Maximise stays on your screen

On Windows with several monitors at different scaling, maximising from the title bar fills the screen that title bar is on, rather than whichever monitor Windows judged held most of the window.

It fits a laptop screen

Everything is drawn at nine tenths of its full size on every screen, so the whole window fits a 13 inch 4K laptop screen running at 300% scaling with nothing cut off at the edge. If you would rather have it larger or smaller, set QT_SCALE_FACTOR before starting it and Stellody uses your size instead of its own.

It can wait by the clock

Stellody can tuck itself down beside the clock and start there when you sign in, so it is ready without interrupting you. Opening it again brings your window back rather than starting a second copy.

The close button asks you once

The first time you close the window, Stellody asks whether that should tuck it away or shut it down properly. Tell it to remember and it never asks again. You can change your mind later.

A guide to the window itself

Help then Guide names every button on both trays beside the picture the window actually draws, so nothing has to be recognised from a description. Under that sit the four rules no single screen can state for itself: your files are only ever read, folders group while tags name, a correction differs from a stated tag, ratings follow the album rather than the file.

How to lay a library out

Stellody reads a collection the way it finds it, so the same guide states the rules it reads by: one album to a folder, discs of one release side by side, two folders joining where the artist and the title both agree, a cue sheet read only where the folder holds one audio file.

The short version is put in front of you before you choose a music folder for the first time, so you can go and look at what you have rather than find out afterwards.

Help, About and updates

The top tray ends with discovery, the light or dark appearance switch and Help. That one Help button drops the same menu as Help on the menu bar: the guide, the library health report, the two licences, About and Check for updates.

Every button is on a menu too

Anything a picture button does can be reached from the menu bar as well, the donation button alone excepted.

File repairs the library and discovers new music; Edit searches and filters; View switches between the sleeves and the list and picks a sleeve size; Sound switches exclusive output, chooses the output device, mutes, shuffles and chooses a repeat mode by name; Control plays, pauses, stops and steps between tracks. A tick beside an entry always says what is really on, however it was switched.
